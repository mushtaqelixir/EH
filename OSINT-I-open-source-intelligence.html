01 Introduction to OSINT

Intelligence that anyone with a browser can collect

Open Source Intelligence — OSINT — is the disciplined collection and analysis of publicly available information to answer a specific question. The material is out in the open; the skill is finding it, verifying it, and connecting it into a conclusion.

There is a ladder every analyst climbs. A single tweet is data. Two hundred tweets from the same account, sorted by time and topic, become information. When you can finally say — with sources, timestamps and a confidence level — "this account was run by the marketing team of company X between March and June," you have produced intelligence. OSINT is not browsing; it is the controlled production of that last rung.

The sources are everywhere: news archives and press releases, social platforms, company registries and court records, property and patent databases, flight and ship trackers, job boards, leaked breach corpora, satellite imagery — even the target's own careers page. Security teams use it to map attacker infrastructure, journalists to verify war footage, recruiters to sanity-check candidates, and red teams to complete the first phase of nearly every engagement without sending a single packet to the target.

RungWhat it looks like
DataA raw fact with no context: one photo, one tweet, one row in a database

REAL LIFE — THE JOB POSTING

A startup whose website mentions only a Berlin office quietly lists a job ad: "Kubernetes engineer, Singapore site." An analyst reads that single public ad and concludes the company is building an APAC data centre — months before any press release. The ad was boring, public, and sitting in plain sight. That is OSINT: nobody broke in; somebody just read carefully.

WHY IT'S POWERFUL

It is cheap, fast, mostly legal, and leaves no footprint on the target — an investigator can map an organisation for weeks without the target's firewalls ever noticing a thing.

WHY IT'S DANGEROUS

Public data can be stale, wrong or deliberately planted. Everything must be verified from two independent sources before it supports a conclusion — or a reputation.

WHO DOES IT

Threat-intel analysts, penetration testers, journalists (Bellingcat), law enforcement, HR and due-diligence teams, election monitors — and, as you'll see in section 10, criminals too. Method is neutral; ethics decide the rest.

02 Where OSINT Fits

One goal, many disciplines: the intelligence family

Nation-scale intelligence is split into "INTs" by how the information is collected. OSINT is the only member of the family whose raw material anyone can lawfully pick up.

For most of the 20th century, OSINT was the junior partner — analysts clipped newspapers while satellites and spy networks did the real work. Today the balance has flipped: so much of life is published online that open sources often supply the majority of the analytic picture, and the classified disciplines are used to fill the remaining gaps. Knowing the family matters because real investigations blend disciplines — an image (IMINT) found by search (OSINT), geolocated by shadows (GEOINT), confirmed by a phone call (HUMINT).

REAL LIFE — THE JOURNALIST'S BADGE

The collective Bellingcat identified the two men accused of the 2018 Skripal poisoning in Salisbury without a single classified source: passport photos in public records, a leaked agency database, car registrations and flight manifests stitched together into a published identification. Everything they used was open — the tradecraft was the difference between noise and proof.

Notice: the report (phase 5) answers the question defined in phase 1 — then raises new questions, which is why the diagram is drawn as a circle, not a line. Real teams run the loop many times on one case.

What makes a good "direction"?

Phase 1 is written down as a set of Priority Information Requirements (PIRs) — short, answerable questions with an owner and a deadline. "Tell me about company X" is not a PIR; "does company X operate internet-facing services outside the EU, and which ones?" is. Every later phase is judged against this list, which is what stops an OSINT project from turning into an endless wiki rabbit hole. Good analysts spend up to a fifth of total effort here, before a single query is typed.

The same rigour applies at the end: a finished report separates fact (with source and time), assessment (analyst's interpretation) and confidence (high / moderate / low). Readers must always be able to tell the evidence from the opinion.

REAL LIFE — THE DEADLINE THAT SAVED A STORY

A newsroom had 48 hours to verify a viral video. Instead of watching clips for two days, the team spent the first evening writing three PIRs: where was it filmed, when, and was it this war or an earlier one? Those three questions cut the search space so sharply that by midnight the shadows and signage had answered all of them — the video was real footage, but from a flood four years earlier. Direction first is what makes speed possible.

04 Collection Posture

Touch nothing — or leave fingerprints

Every collection action sits on a spectrum. Passive techniques never contact the target directly, so the target cannot see you. Active techniques interact with the target and are fast and fresh — but they ring bells.

Passive collection borrows third parties: search-engine caches, the Wayback Machine, public DNS resolvers, certificate-transparency search engines, and databases like Shodan that already scanned the internet last week. The target's logs record traffic from Google, archive.org and Shodan's crawlers — not from you. Active collection sends your packets at their systems: port scans, login probes, staged phone calls. It returns today's truth, but every packet is attributable.

Professional recon almost always runs passive first, squeezing public data until it yields nothing more, and only then going active — inside a written, authorised scope. In a penetration test, the shift from passive to active is a contract boundary: everything before it is invisible to the client's monitoring, everything after it is expected to trigger alerts that the blue team should catch.

05 Search Engine Tradecraft

Google is a database, if you speak its language

Search engines index far more than they show by default. Dorking — advanced query operators — surfaces forgotten directories, unlinked PDFs and exposed admin panels that a plain query would never rank.

Every operator narrows the web by one dimension: where (site:), what type (filetype:), URL shape (inurl:), title (intitle:), body text (intext:) and time (before:/after:). Stack three or four together and a billion pages shrink to a dozen — each one a potential lead. The classic intitle:"index of" alone finds thousands of open directory listings that their owners never meant to publish.

This is the same technique defenders use on themselves: Google your own domain with these operators before an attacker does, and de-index what shouldn't be public. Ethical rule of thumb — dorking your own assets or an authorised scope is diligence; dorking a stranger's systems is where section 10 starts to matter.

REAL LIFE — THE FORGOTTEN BACKUP

A university's old webmaster once dropped a backup.zip into a public folder. It contained the whole 2014 CMS, including a plaintext config file. For six years nobody noticed — but Google indexed the listing, and one dork site:uni.ac.cy intitle:"index of" backup was all it took to surface it. The fix took minutes; the exposure lasted years. Defenders now run these exact queries as routine hygiene.

06 People & Social Footprints

One username is a thread — pull it

People are creatures of habit: the same handle, the same avatar, the same bio line on five platforms. A single unique username can stitch an entire online identity together.

Username enumeration is the fastest pivot in people-OSINT. Tools like Sherlock and WhatsMyName check hundreds of platforms for a given handle in seconds — no login, no request to the person, just profile URLs that either exist or don't. From there, analysts widen the net: who does the account follow, who tags them in photos, which names appear in the same comment threads? Employer and conference bios fill in the offline half — a talk listing ties a handle to a real name, the name to an employer, the employer to an email pattern.

Verification is where amateurs fall off. Common names generate false positives that ruin lives when published; the standard is two independent attributes agreeing (same handle and same city, or same avatar hash and same workplace) before any identification is written down. And the discipline runs both ways: recruiters, banks and journalists routinely "ego-search" their own name to know what's out there — try it on yourself after this section.

REAL LIFE — THE STREAMER'S OLD HANDLE

An anonymous troll account harassed a municipal official for months. An investigator noticed the account's avatar was a cropped frame from a 2017 indie game — and that a Steam user with the same crop had changed usernames twice, leaving a comment trail back to a personal blog signed with the troll's birthday. No hacking, no breach: four public platforms and one habit — never changing the avatar.

HARD LIMIT

People-OSINT is lawful when the material is public and the purpose is legitimate — verification, security assessment, due diligence. It becomes stalking, doxxing or harassment the moment the output is used to intimidate, locate or expose a private person. Same tools, opposite ethics.

07 Infrastructure Recon

Every domain leaves a paper trail

A domain name is a thread tied to registration records, DNS answers, certificate logs and hosting fingerprints. Pull it and an organisation's real internet estate unfolds — often including the parts nobody remembers anymore.

WHOIS / RDAP records show who registered a domain, when, and for how long — a freshly registered look-alike domain ("paypa1-verify.com") is a phishing tell, while a 15-year registration signals an established brand. Privacy redaction hides most personal details today, but the registrar, dates and name servers still speak.

DNS is a free configuration disclosure: A records point at hosting, MX records name the mail provider, TXT records expose SPF rules and third-party verification tokens. Certificate Transparency logs (searchable at crt.sh) list every TLS certificate ever issued for a domain — which means every subdomain anyone ever got a cert for, including the staging box everyone forgot. Feed those into passive-DNS and services like Shodan or Censys, and the picture becomes: which servers, which software versions, which ports — all collected by someone else's scanners weeks ago.

A SaaS company once pointed aws-support.vendorname.com at an EC2 instance, then shut the instance down and let the DNS record lapse — but not the domain trust. 08 Imagery & GEOINT

A photo is a dataset wearing a picture

Images carry structured metadata inside, and physics outside: shadow angles betray the time of day, landmarks betray the place, and search engines betray every earlier appearance of the same picture.

Start inside the file: EXIF metadata can name the camera model, the exact capture timestamp, sometimes GPS coordinates and the editing software. Photos shared through platforms usually have EXIF stripped — but screenshots re-shared carelessly, or files straight from a phone or DSLR, often keep it all. Reverse image search (Google Lens, TinEye, Yandex) then answers the oldest question in verification: has this picture existed before this claim was made?

Outside the file, physics takes over. Shadow analysis — direction and length, checked against tools like SunCalc — puts an approximate time and latitude on any outdoor photo. Vegetation, road markings, signage and mountain skylines narrow the location; matching them against satellite imagery in Google Earth closes it. This is chronolocation, and it is how a single frame gets pinned to a street corner within minutes.

Professional verification runs a fixed checklist: provenance (who shot it and where did you get it), source (is the uploader first-hand), date (metadata, weather, shadows), location (landmarks, geolocation) and motive (why is it being shared now). A claim must survive all five — not just the ones it passes.

REAL LIFE — THE WRONG-CITY RALLY

During a 2020 election, a photo of an enormous rally circulated with the caption "yesterday in our city." Reverse image search found the identical photo on stock-agency sites — shot at a 2015 concert 4,000 km away. Total verification time: ninety seconds, one search. Modern misinformation rarely survives a full checklist; it survives not being checked.

10 Ethics, Law & Self-Defence

Just because it's public doesn't mean it's a free-for-all

OSINT sits on a line most people never see. On one side: verification, security and journalism. On the other: harassment and stalking that used the identical techniques. What separates them is authorization, proportion and restraint.

The legal floor. Publicly accessible is not the same as free to exploit: unauthorised access beyond what a system offers the public is a crime almost everywhere (the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act in the US, the IT Act in India, comparable statutes across the EU), and data-protection law — GDPR, India's DPDP Act 2023 — restricts how personal data may be processed regardless of where it was found. Platform terms of service add another layer, and anti-stalking law applies to collection "just for curiosity." Engaged investigations need a written scope and authorisation before the first query; personal projects need the minimum-necessary principle — collect only what the question requires, and stop when it is answered.

Your own footprint. The investigator is also a target. Research from your main browser and the target's analytics learn you instantly: your accounts, your interests, your IP. Standard tradecraft is a dedicated alias identity with its own email and avatar, a separate browser profile or VM, a VPN or Tor for anything sensitive, and above all non-interaction — never like, follow, reply or visit a profile from a personal account, because every interaction is a notification to the other side that someone is looking. Log every query as you make it: your future self (or your lawyer) will need the trail.

THE GOLDEN RULE

Before any collection step, ask: could I explain this action, out loud, in open court — to the person I investigated? If yes, proceed and document. If no, the technique was the problem, not the target.
